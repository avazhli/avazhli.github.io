Ethical dilemmas in data science

Convenience and Control in Facial Recognition Technology

Facial recognition technology, initially restricted to military and law enforcement surveillance, has quickly expanded into everyday consumer applications, from airport trusted traveler programs to simply unlocking your phone. These biometric systems often rely on machine learning models and neural networks trained on millions of facial images to map facial geometries, classify demographic traits, and recognize individuals across different environments. Their performance depends heavily on the quality, lighting, and demographic composition of the datasets used to train and test them. The need for massive image databases introduces ethical dilemmas, both in how the countless facial images are sourced and in the deployment of the resulting systems.

To begin to examine these issues, this case study focuses on the Facial Recognition Technology Evaluation (FRTE), the “gold-standard” national benchmark test administered by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) to evaluate commercial facial recognition algorithms. Dr. Nina Toft Djanegara explains how the benchmark datasets were created using unconsented visa application photos and border detainee mugshots. I then use Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru (2018) and Simone Browne (2010) on bias in facial analysis and the racialization of biometric surveillance as a more theoretical and critical lens to analyze the case.

Informed Consent and Data Sourcing

Informed consent requires data subjects to fully understand how their personal information will be collected and used, and to have the autonomy to opt in or out. Toft Djanegara explains that the initial FRTE dataset included around 1,200 volunteers who explicitly consented to being photographed. However, as the technology rapidly developed, NIST needed new images to better evaluate the programs and replaced the initial dataset with the HCInt dataset, composed of 121,589 Mexican visa application photos from the U.S. Department of State. These applicants never agreed to participate, and they had no real choice in the matter, since declining to submit a photo means not receiving a visa. As facial recognition programs continue to improve and new images are needed, NIST continues to draw data from readily available, pre-existing government databases, disproportionately using the faces of immigrants. The FRTE now includes 6.2 million global visa photos and Central American border detainee mugshots.

This process of capturing data is not limited to NIST. Toft Djanegara finds that commercial firms such as Clearview AI and Everalbum have also trained law enforcement and military models on billions of personal photos scraped from online platforms without users’ awareness. Across these examples, the people whose faces the facial recognition systems rely on are rarely informed or given a choice. For non-citizens in particular, Toft Djanegara describes a tension in which their digitized “data body,” created through mandatory photographs and identification by the state, continues to circulate, even when their physical body is denied entry.

Unintended Data Use

Beyond how the data was obtained, the ways data is extracted from its original context and put to new uses create further ethical concerns. “Function creep,” as Toft Djanegara explains, occurs when data gathered for a specific purpose is repurposed for broader applications. In this case, photographs submitted strictly for visa document verification and bureaucratic processing were repurposed by NIST to create evaluation benchmarks that rank commercial facial recognition algorithms. These rankings and scores are then used by private companies to win government contracts to develop and implement systems to identify others.

Toft Djanegara conceptualizes this as “imperial recycling,” in which state agencies extract value from marginalized non-citizens whose data remains on government servers at the state’s discretion, even if their visa applications are declined. Browne similarly describes an “identity-industrial complex,” in which body data and identification management are monetized while the people they belong to are denied ownership of their own data. The harm, then, extends beyond the initial collection of data; the uses of the data afterward have their own consequences. The data has been repurposed for facial recognition technologies that are used to identify, sort, and control bodies. The original purpose, verifying one applicant’s identity, has ironically become the foundation for a new technology that sorts and identifies others, at the expense of the non-citizens whose images were taken without their knowledge and to the benefit of private corporations and the state.

Representativeness and Racial Categorization

Another question considers who was measured, and whether those individuals are representative of the people to whom the algorithm will be applied. Dataset representativeness requires data to reflect real-world populations accurately in order to prevent biased performance. Toft Djanegara explains that the early FRTE datasets lacked diversity, so to remedy this, NIST turned to more databases of “non-white” individuals. U.S. government immigrant photo databases were especially valuable because they were explicitly labeled with important details about the subjects. But a need for more “diverse” data does not have a simple solution, because the race and ethnicity labels themselves are unstable.

Race, as a social construct, is not something algorithms can directly measure. While there may be some phenotypic commonality within racial groups, there is no clear way to characterize and define these categories. NIST’s datasets equated nationality and race, using skin pigmentation and nationality as proxies. Toft Djanegara explains that in doing so, the creators of these technologies are in effect reifying racial categories and assuming racial homogeneity within national boundaries. The representativeness of a dataset is important; however, achieving it is complicated, because beyond the challenge of obtaining the data consensually, the categories used to define “diversity” are themselves imprecise and constructed.

Data Values Manifesto: Recognizing and Mitigating Bias in Data

One of the values in the Data Values and Principles manifesto is to “recognize and mitigate bias in ourselves and in the data we use.” This requires data teams to acknowledge that algorithms reflect the biases of the creators and to actively work to address them through the data used and the systemic design. Browne describes a “white prototypicality” embedded in facial recognition software, where whiteness is made normative. Camera sensors and software are often optimized for lighter skin, producing errors as algorithms struggle to parse darker skin, irises, or certain Asian features. Buolamwini and Gebru measure this effect by evaluating commercial classifiers on their Pilot Parliaments Benchmark (PPB), finding higher error rates for female subjects and darker-skinned subjects, with error rates up to almost 35% for darker-skinned females compared to 0.0% to 0.8% for lighter-skinned males.

The question then becomes: how do we attempt to mitigate these biases? One argument is to include more diverse datasets; however, it is important to consider whose photos are being used and how they are collected. Toft Djanegara critiques NIST’s approach of accumulating non-white immigrant photos, arguing that it treats human faces as extractable raw material, “faces in the wild,” and a commodity, rather than as people with a say in how their images are used. Mitigating bias this way seemingly undermines the manifesto’s goal, since the groups least accurately recognized are often the same ones whose data is taken without consent.

Why It Matters: Benefits, Harms, Power

In evaluating this technology, I think it is important to consider the manifesto’s value of “use data to improve life for our users, customers, organizations, and communities.” The incorporation of biometric technologies into everyday applications raises tensions between convenience, privacy, control, and power. These technologies may seem to make our lives more convenient, but at what cost, and at whose convenience?

Commercial technology vendors and the state benefit both financially and operationally. Companies use government benchmark rankings like NIST’s FRTE to secure lucrative government contracts, converting captured and repurposed human data into profit and increased state power. Darker-skinned individuals, women, immigrants, and non-citizens bear a great portion of the burden in this case study. Not only is their data being harvested without their knowledge or choice, but they are being racialized and categorized in the effort to build and train these systems, becoming commodities that support the same systems later used to surveil and control their own communities. These ethical violations in the data used to train and test facial recognition programs are driven by a combination of commercial profit and state surveillance power. As we continue to incorporate these technologies into our lives, it is important to continue to consider the ethical implications and trade-offs.
